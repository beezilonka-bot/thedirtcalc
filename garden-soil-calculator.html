How much garden soil does an in-ground bed need?

This calculator is for the open garden — beds and rows dug into the ground — as opposed to the framed boxes covered by the raised bed calculator. The difference matters for the numbers. A raised bed starts empty and is filled to its full depth with bought mix. An in-ground bed starts with native soil that is already there, so most projects are adding a layer, not filling a void: a new bed typically gets 4–6 inches of garden soil or a soil–compost blend worked into the loosened top of the native ground, and an established bed being refreshed gets 2–3 inches. Enter the depth you are actually adding, not the depth of the root zone you wish you had.

The standard volume math applies — length × width × depth ÷ 27 = cubic yards — with one garden-specific twist: amendment math. Gardeners often work in ratios: to build a new bed over poor ground, a common recipe is roughly half existing loosened soil, half added blend, with 2–3 inches of straight compost on top in the first year. If your plan is compost only, run it at that depth with the density set to 40; if it is a blend, keep the default density. Either way, the recommended order adds 10% because loose soil settles into the native ground as soon as it is watered and walked on.

Quality beats quantity in a vegetable bed, because the soil is the fertilizer programme. A blend with real compost in it (you should be able to see dark, crumbly organic matter, not just brown dirt) will out-grow a larger volume of plain topsoil. Four inches of good blend beats eight inches of cheap fill; if the budget forces a choice, put the good material where the heavy feeders (tomatoes, squash, brassicas) will grow.

Worked example: a 20 ft × 30 ft vegetable garden, adding 6 inches of blend

A 20 ft × 30 ft plot is 600 sq ft. Adding 6 inches (0.5 ft) of garden soil blend gives 600 × 0.5 = 300 cubic feet, or 11.11 cubic yards. At the blend density of 45 lb per cubic foot that weighs about 13,500 lb, or 6.75 tons — a bulk delivery, since bagged blend would take 200 of the 1.5 cu ft bags. With the 10% settling buffer, order 12.22 cubic yards. Note the contrast with a framed garden of the same footprint: four 4 × 8 ft raised beds at 12 inches deep would need only about 4.74 cubic yards of mix, because the paths between beds are not filled.

Common mistakes

  • Calculating an in-ground bed like an empty box. Your native soil is still there. Ordering the full root-zone depth as bought soil doubles or triples the real requirement — enter only the depth you are adding.
  • Filling the paths too. In a bed-and-path layout, only the beds get good soil. Measuring the whole plot when a third of it is paths inflates the order by that third.
  • Buying plain topsoil and calling it garden soil. Topsoil alone is low in organic matter and nutrients. Vegetables want a blend with substantial compost; if you buy topsoil, budget separately for 2–3 inches of compost to work in.
  • Working soil when it is wet. Digging and tilling saturated soil destroys its structure and creates hard clods that persist all season. Add and work your new soil when the ground is moist, not muddy — the volume math assumes it spreads like soil, not like cement.

Frequently asked questions

How much soil do I need for a vegetable garden?

For an in-ground bed, calculate only the layer you are adding: area × added depth ÷ 27. A 20 × 30 ft garden getting 6 inches of blend needs 11.11 cubic yards. Refreshing an existing bed at 2–3 inches needs roughly a third to half of that.

What is the difference between garden soil and raised bed mix?

They are close cousins — both are topsoil-and-compost blends. Raised bed mix is blended lighter and fluffier (often with bark or perlite-style aeration) because a box has no native soil to lean on and drainage is critical. In-ground garden soil can be a denser, cheaper blend because it marries into the soil below.

How much compost should I add to a garden bed?

A 2–3 inch layer worked into the top of the bed is the standard annual amendment for an established garden; new beds on poor ground often start with 3–4 inches. At 2 inches deep, one cubic yard of compost covers about 162 sq ft.

Should I fill my whole garden plot or just the beds?

Just the beds. Paths can stay native soil, gravel or mulch. In a typical layout beds are 60–70% of the plot, so measuring bed area only — or using this calculator's second area for separate bed blocks — cuts the soil bill by a third.

How deep should the soil be for vegetables?

Most vegetables root happily in 8–12 inches of decent soil over loosened ground; deep-rooted crops like carrots and parsnips want the full 12. In-ground, that depth is native soil plus what you add — you do not need to buy all 12 inches.
