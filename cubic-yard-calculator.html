How cubic yards are calculated

Every bulk landscape material — soil, mulch, gravel, sand, stone — is bought and sold by the cubic yard, a volume equal to a cube 3 ft on each side, or 27 cubic feet. The formula behind this calculator is the one every other calculator here is built on: cubic yards = (length in ft × width in ft × depth in ft) ÷ 27. Depth is the step people trip on: it is usually measured in inches, so divide inches by 12 first (4 inches = 0.33 ft). One yard at a given depth covers a fixed area, which makes a handy cross-check: 1 cubic yard covers 324 sq ft at 1 inch deep, 162 sq ft at 2 inches, 108 at 3 inches, 81 at 4 inches, 54 at 6 inches and 27 sq ft at a full foot deep.

Volume is only half the answer. Suppliers selling by the ton, and anyone hauling material in their own truck, also needs weight — and weight depends entirely on the material. Multiply cubic feet by the density in pounds per cubic foot and divide by 2,000 to get tons. The material picker above exists because there is no single “dirt weight”: the same 7.41 cubic yards that weigh 7.5 tons as topsoil would weigh barely 2.5 tons as mulch and over 10 tons as crushed stone.

Finally there are bags. Bagged material is labelled in cubic feet — commonly 0.75 or 1.5 cu ft for soil and mulch, 0.5 cu ft for stone — so bag count is simply cubic feet divided by bag size, rounded up. As the worked example shows, the bag count explodes fast, which is why bulk delivery usually wins past about 2 cubic yards; the bulk vs. bagged calculator on this site runs that price comparison with your local numbers.

Worked example: 20 ft × 30 ft area, 4 inches deep

A 20 ft × 30 ft rectangle is 600 sq ft. Four inches of depth is 0.33 ft, so the volume is 600 × 0.33 = 200 cubic feet, and 200 ÷ 27 = 7.41 cubic yards. With topsoil selected (75 lb per cubic foot) that volume weighs about 15,000 lb, or 7.50 tons. In bags it works out to 267 of the 0.75 cu ft size or 134 of the 1.5 cu ft size — a clear case for a bulk delivery. Add the 10% buffer for settling and uneven ground and the recommended order is 8.15 cubic yards. Switch the material to mulch and watch the same space drop to about 2.5 tons: the yardage never changes, only the weight does.

Common mistakes

  • Entering depth in feet instead of inches. A “4” in the depth box means 4 inches here. Typing feet into an inch field (or the reverse on other sites) is the single most common cause of 12× over-orders.
  • Using one density for every material. Treating mulch like stone overstates weight four-fold; treating stone like soil understates it by a third. Pick the actual material before quoting tonnage to a supplier or loading a trailer.
  • Forgetting that a cubic yard is 27 cubic feet, not 9. Nine is square feet in a square yard — an area measure. Mixing the two shrinks your order to a third of what you need.
  • Measuring a sloped or irregular area as one rectangle. Break odd shapes into rectangles, circles and triangles and add them up — this calculator has a second-area option for exactly that.

Frequently asked questions

How do I convert square feet and depth to cubic yards?

Multiply length × width in feet to get square feet, multiply by the depth in feet (inches ÷ 12), then divide by 27. Example: 600 sq ft at 4 inches deep is 600 × 0.33 ÷ 27 ≈ 7.41 cubic yards.

How many cubic feet are in a cubic yard?

27 cubic feet. A cubic yard is a 3 ft × 3 ft × 3 ft cube, and 3 × 3 × 3 = 27. Bagged products are labelled in cubic feet, so divide your cubic footage by the bag size to get a bag count.

How much does a cubic yard weigh?

It depends entirely on the material: roughly 675 lb for shredded mulch, about 1,100 lb for compost, about 2,025 lb for screened topsoil, and around 2,700 lb for crushed stone or sand. That is why this calculator asks you to pick a material instead of giving one generic weight.

How many bags equal a cubic yard?

36 bags at 0.75 cu ft, 18 bags at 1.5 cu ft, or 54 bags at 0.5 cu ft (the common size for bagged stone). Past roughly 2 cubic yards, delivered bulk is usually cheaper and always less work.

Should I round my cubic yard result up or down?

Up, slightly. Loose material settles and compacts after watering and traffic, and suppliers sell in whole or half yards. This calculator's recommended order adds 10%, which lands most projects within one wheelbarrow of perfect.
