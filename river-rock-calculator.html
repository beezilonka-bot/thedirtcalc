Quick answer

A 3 ft × 20 ft foundation bed covered 3 inches deep needs 0.56 cubic yards of river rock — about 0.68 tons, or exactly 20 bags of 0.75 cu ft. With the 10% buffer, order about 0.75 tons, or 22 bags.

  • The math: volume = length × width × depth (depth in feet); cubic yards = cubic feet ÷ 27; tons = cubic feet × density ÷ 2,000.
  • Density used: 90 lb per cubic foot (1.22 tons per cubic yard) for river rock — the lightest of the three stone types per yard.
  • Bag counts are rounded up and given for both standard bag sizes — 0.75 cu ft and 1.5 cu ft (one cubic yard = 36 bags of 0.75 cu ft, or 18 bags of 1.5 cu ft).

How to calculate river rock

The formula is area × depth ÷ 27 for cubic yards, converted at about 1.22 tons per yard. Depth for river rock is driven by stone size: the layer should be at least twice the diameter of the stone so pieces nest and cover the fabric beneath. For common 1–2 inch rock that means about 3 inches deep; for chunky 3-inch rock, 4 inches. A single shallow layer looks good on day one and shows fabric by month three as stones shift.

River rock jobs are usually decorative — foundation beds, edging bands, dry creek beds, drainage swales — so shape matters. Creek beds are rarely rectangles: split an L-shaped or curved run into two or three rectangular sections and use the second-area option (and re-run for a third section, adding the totals). For a round tree ring or a circular accent bed, switch the shape to Circle and enter the diameter. Under all of it goes landscape fabric: river rock on bare soil slowly sinks and mixes with dirt, and fabric also keeps the stones cleaner-looking.

Buying format is the last decision. Bagged river rock (typically 0.5 cu ft, about 45–50 lb) suits edging and small accents; a pallet or bulk ton suits creek beds and full foundation plantings. Because the stones are heavy and the bags small, the bag count climbs fast — the worked example below needs 20 bags for a modest strip bed. The recommended order adds 10%: river rock settles into its voids after the first few rains, and having a small reserve pile makes future top-ups match in color.

Worked example: 3 ft × 20 ft foundation bed, 3 inches deep

A 3 ft × 20 ft bed is 60 sq ft. At 3 inches (0.25 ft) deep the volume is 60 × 0.25 = 15 cubic feet = 0.56 cubic yards. At 90 lb per cubic foot that weighs 1,350 lb ≈ 0.68 tons. In 0.75 cu ft bags it is exactly 20 bags — a good illustration of why anything past one bed usually goes bulk. With the settling buffer, order about 0.75 tons or 22 bags.

Common mistakes

  • One stone deep. A single layer shifts and exposes fabric. Depth should be at least twice the stone's diameter — about 3 inches for typical 1–2 inch rock.
  • No fabric underneath. River rock pressed into soil by rain and foot traffic disappears into the ground over a few seasons. Fabric keeps your calculated layer on top where it belongs.
  • Measuring a creek bed as one rectangle. Curved, varying-width runs need to be split into sections. Averaging the width of a winding creek bed underestimates the wide bends.
  • Expecting river rock to work like mulch around plants. Stone heats up, reflects heat onto foliage and adds no organic matter. Keep it in decorative beds and drainage features, and give heat-sensitive plants soil and mulch instead.

Frequently asked questions

How much does a cubic yard of river rock weigh?

About 2,430 lb — 1.22 tons — at the 90 lb/cu ft average used here. Larger stone sizes have slightly more void space and can run a touch lighter per yard.

How deep should river rock be?

At least twice the stone diameter: about 3 inches for 1–2 inch rock, 4 inches for 3 inch rock. Dry creek beds often go 4–6 inches in the channel center.

How much area does a ton of river rock cover?

One ton is about 22 cu ft: roughly 89 sq ft at 3 inches deep, or 67 sq ft at 4 inches.

How many bags of river rock do I need?

It depends on the job size — the calculator gives exact counts for 0.75 and 1.5 cu ft bags. As a feel: a 3×20 ft bed at 3 inches needs 20 of the 0.75 cu ft bags (about 0.68 tons).

River rock or mulch for foundation beds?

River rock never needs replacing and sheds water, but costs more up front, runs hot, and is miserable to plant in later. Mulch is cheaper, plant-friendly and needs annual top-ups. Many homes use rock in narrow, plant-free strips and mulch where things grow.
