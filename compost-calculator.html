How to calculate compost

Compost depth is the whole decision. As a top-dressing on garden beds, 1–2 inches worked in or laid on top is the standard annual feeding. On a lawn, ¼–½ inch screened compost spread and raked in is plenty — more will smother grass. When blending raised bed mix (roughly 30% compost by volume), calculate the bed's total volume with the raised bed calculator and take about a third of it as compost. For amending a new bed from scratch, 2–3 inches tilled into the top 6–8 inches of soil is a common target.

The volume math is the usual area × depth ÷ 27 for cubic yards. Compost compresses far more than soil — a fluffy dumped yard shrinks noticeably once spread and watered — and it continues to break down in place, which is the point of using it. The recommended order adds 10% for that shrinkage. If you are buying bagged compost (commonly 1 or 1.5 cu ft bags), note that bagged material is usually drier and lighter per cubic foot than bulk, so bag counts here use volume, not weight — the right way to compare them.

One coverage shortcut worth knowing: one cubic yard of compost covers about 160 sq ft at 2 inches deep, 320 sq ft at 1 inch, and over 1,200 sq ft at lawn top-dressing depth (¼ inch). If your calculated yardage looks surprisingly small for a lawn, that is why — thin layers over big areas are the norm for compost, not the exception.

Worked example: 20 ft × 10 ft vegetable beds, 2 inches deep

Beds totaling 20 ft × 10 ft cover 200 sq ft. At 2 inches (0.167 ft) deep the volume is 200 × 0.167 = 33.3 cubic feet = 1.23 cubic yards of compost. In bags that is about 23 of the 1.5 cu ft size — right at the tipping point where a bulk yard delivered starts to win on price. The compost weighs roughly 1,333 lb (0.67 tons). With shrinkage covered, order 1.36 cubic yards in bulk or about 25 bags.

Common mistakes

  • Treating compost like mulch. A 3–4 inch compost blanket wastes money and can overload soil with nutrients and salts. Compost is food, not cover — 1–2 inches is a full dose for beds.
  • Smothering a lawn with a thick layer. More than about ½ inch over grass blocks light and mats down. Screened compost at ¼–½ inch, raked so blades poke through, is correct.
  • Planting straight into pure compost. Most compost is too rich and loose as a sole growing medium. Blend it into soil or use it as a top-dressing, except for heavy feeders in short-season containers.
  • Comparing bag prices by weight. Bagged compost is sold by volume (cu ft) and varies in moisture. A heavier bag is often just a wetter bag — compare price per cubic foot.

Frequently asked questions

How much compost do I need per square foot?

At 1 inch deep, about 0.0031 cu yd per sq ft — so 100 sq ft needs roughly 0.31 cu yd (8.3 cu ft). At 2 inches, double it: about 0.62 cu yd per 100 sq ft.

How many bags of compost are in a cubic yard?

Twenty-seven 1 cu ft bags, 18 bags at 1.5 cu ft, or 36 at 0.75 cu ft — one cubic yard is always 27 cubic feet regardless of material.

How much area does one yard of compost cover?

About 324 sq ft at 1 inch deep, 162 sq ft at 2 inches, and roughly 1,296 sq ft at a ¼-inch lawn top-dressing.

How much does a cubic yard of compost weigh?

Around 1,000–1,200 lb (about half a ton) at typical moisture — this calculator uses 1,080 lb. Manure-based compost can be heavier, leaf compost lighter.

Compost or topsoil — which do I need?

Topsoil adds depth and shape; compost adds biology and nutrients. Raising a bed's level is a topsoil job; feeding existing soil is a compost job. Many gardeners do both: topsoil for depth, 1–2 inches of compost on top.
